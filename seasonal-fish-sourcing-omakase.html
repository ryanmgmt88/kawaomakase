How We Choose What's on the Menu: Seasonal Fish & the Art of Sourcing

By Chef Tony, KAWA Omakase · East Village, New York City

People ask me what's on the menu next week and I genuinely can't tell them. Not because we're being mysterious — because I don't know yet. The menu at KAWA is decided by what arrives, what looks exceptional, and what's at peak quality right now. This is how omakase is supposed to work, and it starts long before the fish reaches the counter.

The morning routine

Most days I'm in contact with our suppliers before the restaurant opens. That means calls, texts, and sometimes walking through the market in the early hours. In New York City, the Fulton Fish Market in the Bronx is the main wholesale hub — the largest fish market in the United States — and it runs from the early morning hours. You need to be there early, or you need a relationship with someone who is.

Over 20 years of working in New York Japanese restaurants, I've built those relationships. The dealers I buy from know what I'm looking for and call me when something exceptional arrives — a particularly clean bluefin, an unusual delivery of live scallops, a run of exceptional wild striped bass. That trust is what makes the menu possible.

"The best ingredient decision is always the one you make at the market at 5am, not at the desk the night before."

How seasons change the menu

Japanese culinary culture has always been deeply attuned to seasonality — the concept of shun (旬) refers to the peak moment of an ingredient, the window when it's at its absolute finest. For fish, that window can be very short.

Spring

Cherry blossom sea bream (sakura dai), young yellowtail, surf clam, firefly squid, white fish transitioning from winter richness

Summer

Lean bluefin (akami), Japanese sea bass, squid, bonito, live abalone, fresh water eel (unagi)

Autumn

Fatty tuna season begins, Pacific saury (sanma), salmon, mackerel (saba) at peak fat content, sea urchin from Hokkaido

Winter

Fatty bluefin toro, snow crab, monkfish liver (ankimo), oysters, flounder (hirame) — the richest season

These aren't arbitrary categories — the fat content, texture, and flavour of the same species changes significantly across the year. A mackerel in October, when it's been feeding all summer, is a completely different eating experience from a mackerel in April. Serving something out of its season is like serving an unripe tomato and calling it a salad.

Local versus imported fish

A common question: does omakase require Japanese fish to be authentic? The honest answer is no — and the most honest omakase is one that serves what's excellent rather than what's prestigious.

New York has exceptional local fish. Striped bass from Long Island, fluke from the East Coast, bluefin tuna caught off the coast of Nova Scotia — these are world-class fish, and some of the best tuna served at New York omakase counters never leaves the Western hemisphere. We supplement with Japanese imports when they're genuinely superior — Hokkaido uni, certain yellowtail, specific seasonal items — but we don't import for the sake of a label.

The rice question

Fish gets most of the attention in omakase conversations, but experienced diners will tell you that rice is where chefs really reveal themselves. Sushi rice — shari — needs to be warm (not hot, not cold), seasoned precisely with the right balance of rice vinegar, salt, and sugar, and textured so each grain retains integrity while the block holds together in your hand.

I've been adjusting my rice formula for 20 years. It changes slightly with humidity, with the season, with the specific rice crop. It's one of those things that looks simple from the outside and is genuinely demanding to get right consistently.

Why this means the menu is never fixed

If you've eaten at KAWA more than once, you'll have noticed the menu is different each time. Sometimes significantly — a whole different protein in the canapé, a different sequence of the nigiri, a new fish entirely. This is not inconsistency; it's the entire point. We're not executing a fixed program. We're responding to what's available and excellent right now, every single service.

This is what separates omakase from a tasting menu with a permanent structure. The chef is making real decisions, not re-running a choreography. When it works, guests taste that — there's an aliveness to the meal that's hard to describe but easy to feel.
