Best Omakase in NYC Under $100: What Makes KAWA Different

By Chef Tony, KAWA Omakase · East Village, New York City

New York City has some of the finest omakase dining in the world outside Japan. It also has some of the most expensive. Many well-regarded counters charge $200 to $400 per person before drinks or service — a legitimate barrier for most diners who are simply curious about the format. At KAWA, we've built something different: a 14-course seasonal omakase for $88. Here's how, and why it doesn't mean compromising on what matters.

The NYC omakase price landscape

To understand what $88 means in context, it helps to map the NYC omakase spectrum. At the top end, reservation-only counters in Midtown and the West Village command $300–$450 per person, driven by imported Japanese fish flown in daily, ultra-premium real estate, and extensive beverage programs. The mid-tier — roughly $130–$200 — covers most of the well-reviewed counters in Manhattan and Brooklyn. Under $100 is genuinely rare for a full omakase sequence.

How we keep the price at $88

The honest answer involves three things: location, relationships, and no waste.

Location. East Village rents, while not cheap, are meaningfully lower than Midtown or the West Village. We're not paying to be adjacent to a hotel lobby or a fashion boutique. That cost saving passes directly to the price of the seat.

Supplier relationships. Chef Tony has been buying fish in New York City for over 20 years. Those relationships — built at Catch NYC, Sushi Dojo, Sushi on Jones, and elsewhere — mean access to the same quality fish at better prices than a newer restaurant can negotiate. Trust compounds in this business.

Seasonal flexibility. We don't lock into a fixed menu. The omakase changes constantly based on what's excellent that week — sometimes that's bluefin from the North Atlantic, sometimes it's striped bass from Long Island. Chasing peak quality rather than prestige-priced labels keeps costs rational without sacrificing the eating experience.

"The best fish isn't always the most expensive fish — it's the fish that arrived this morning."

What $88 gets you at KAWA

Fourteen courses: two appetizers, ten pieces of nigiri pressed to order at the counter, a canapé, and a handroll to close. Every piece of nigiri is cut and seasoned individually by Chef Tony — there's no pre-sliced fish sitting in a refrigerated case. The rice is warm and seasoned precisely, which makes an enormous difference to the eating experience that most first-time omakase guests don't anticipate.

The counter seats a small number of guests per sitting, which means the meal feels genuinely intimate — not like a conveyor-belt omakase experience scaled up for volume. Chef Tony explains each course as it arrives. Questions are welcome.

Is affordable omakase actually good?

Price and quality in omakase are not as correlated as the market sometimes suggests. What determines quality at the counter is primarily: the chef's training and taste, the freshness and sourcing of the fish, and the care taken with rice. None of those things require a $300 price point.

What higher-priced counters often offer is a greater variety of imported or ultra-premium species — fatty tuna from specific farms, live shellfish, aged fish. Those are real differences, and they're reflected in the price. But for most diners — including experienced omakase guests — a well-executed $88 menu with excellent seasonal fish will be a more satisfying meal than a perfunctory $200 one.
