What Is Omakase? A First-Timer's Guide to Dining at the Sushi Counter

By Chef Tony, KAWA Omakase · East Village, New York City

If you've ever felt intimidated walking into a Japanese restaurant and seeing "omakase" on the menu with no further explanation — you're not alone. The word gets used a lot in New York City dining, sometimes loosely. Here's what it actually means, what to expect when you sit down at an omakase counter, and how to make the most of the experience.

What does "omakase" mean?

Omakase (お任せ) is a Japanese phrase that translates roughly as "I leave it up to you" or "I trust you." When you order omakase at a restaurant, you're handing full control of the menu to the chef. There is no à la carte selection, no picking from a list. You sit down, and the chef decides what you eat — based on what's freshest, what's in season, and what tells the best story that evening.

At its heart, omakase is a relationship between chef and guest. It requires real trust — and at a good omakase counter, that trust is rewarded with a meal you couldn't have ordered yourself.

"Omakase is not just a meal format. It's a conversation between the chef and the season."

What happens at an omakase counter?

Most omakase restaurants, including KAWA, seat guests at a counter directly facing the chef. This is intentional — you're not just eating, you're watching. Every piece of nigiri is pressed in front of you. Every sauce is applied by hand. Every course arrives with a brief explanation of what it is and where it came from.

A typical omakase progression might look like this: the meal begins with a few small appetizer courses — called sakizuke or tsumami — that warm up the palate and signal the season. Then comes the main event: a procession of nigiri, usually 8–12 pieces, each served one or two at a time. The meal closes with a handroll, a small dessert, or both.

At KAWA, our 14-course menu includes 2 appetizers, 10 pieces of nigiri, a canapé, and a handroll. Every element is designed to flow — lighter fish first, richer cuts later, the handroll as a warm, satisfying finish.

How do you eat omakase? Is there etiquette?

The most important rule: eat each piece as soon as it's placed in front of you. Nigiri is served at the perfect temperature and seasoned precisely — letting it sit changes the texture and dilutes the flavour. The chef has timed every piece; honour that timing.

You can eat nigiri with your hands or chopsticks — both are perfectly acceptable in Japan and at our counter. If you use chopsticks, pick up the piece gently and eat it in one or two bites. You generally do not need to add extra soy sauce — the chef has already seasoned the fish. If soy sauce is offered, use it sparingly, and dip the fish (not the rice) if you do.

Beyond that: ask questions. The counter is not a formal, silent ceremony. Chef Tony and the team are happy to explain every fish, where it came from, and how it was prepared. Curiosity makes the meal better.

What should you tell the chef before the meal?

Always disclose allergies and dietary restrictions when you make your reservation, and again when you arrive. A shellfish allergy, an aversion to sea urchin, a preference to avoid certain fish — none of these are unusual, and a good chef will work around them without making it a big deal.

What you don't need to do: show up with a list of preferences about what you "like" and "don't like." Part of the joy of omakase is being surprised. Trust the chef to introduce you to something you might not have ordered yourself.

Is omakase expensive?

In New York City, omakase can range from around $80 to well over $400 per person. The price generally reflects the quality and rarity of the fish, the experience level of the chef, and the overhead of the space. High-end omakase counters in Midtown or the West Village routinely charge $150–$250 before drinks.

At KAWA, we offer a 14-course seasonal omakase for $88 per guest. We're able to keep the price accessible because of our East Village location and because Chef Tony has spent two decades building the supplier relationships that get us the same quality fish without the uptown markup.
